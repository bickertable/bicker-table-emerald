Buyers Search for a Word You May Never Have Used.

Her website says coach. Her LinkedIn says advisor. Her bio says former executive. A hiring manager types “fractional COO” into a search box, and nothing she owns uses those words.

BICKER TABLE ADVISORY · AUTHORITY SERIES · ESSAY 02

Picture a business coach who held three chief officer roles before she began coaching. She ran operations for a private equity-backed company. She built finance functions from scratch at two startups. She sat at the leadership table through every kind of organizational crisis a company can have. Now she coaches senior leaders, takes the occasional strategy engagement, and carries three client relationships she started before anyone called that kind of work anything in particular.

Her website says coach. Her LinkedIn says advisor. Her bio says former executive.

A hiring manager at a growth-stage company types "fractional COO" into a search box. Nothing the coach owns uses those words.

The Word Belongs to the Buyer Now

The label "fractional executive" moved into common business use fast. A figure repeated across industry write-ups, including SHRM's Executive Network, says LinkedIn profiles mentioning fractional leadership grew from about 2,000 in 2022 to more than 110,000 by early 2024. I could not find the original LinkedIn data, so I treat it as a sign of direction and not a measurement. The direction is clear. Buyers adopted a vocabulary. Many professionals who do the work never did.

Buyers search for a word you may never have used.

That is a structural problem, not a personal failing. A buyer sits down with a budget and a mandate. They need a revenue leader two days a week. They type a role name into a search engine, a LinkedIn filter, or an AI assistant. "Fractional CRO." "Fractional CMO." "Fractional CFO." If you have done that work under different language, there is little for the search to match. The buyer finds someone whose vocabulary fits theirs, whether or not their experience fits yours.

The Usual Advice Assumes the Buyer Knows You

The usual advice is to stay true to your own brand language. Develop your framework. Use the words that feel authentic to your practice. That advice is not wrong. Relationships matter, and referrals carry most of the work. Go Fractional surveyed 213 fractional professionals in April 2026, and 92 percent said they rely primarily on personal networks to find clients. Go Fractional operates a commercial fractional talent platform, so I read the figure as a signal and not a measurement of the whole market.

That number is the problem hiding inside the advice. If most work moves through people who already know you, the buyer outside that circle has only a search to go on. In the same survey, 90 percent named client acquisition as a challenge. The survey does not say why. It points to the same place.

Your results and your relationships are real. A buyer cannot search in your language.

A Missing Word Is an Infrastructure Problem

This is not an argument for abandoning how you think about your work. Call yourself a strategic advisor in every conversation you have. Structure your engagements as you always have. The point is narrower. Search engines index text, and LinkedIn search matches words in profiles. An AI assistant answering a buyer's question draws on text it can find about you. If the word "fractional" appears in none of your headline, summary, website copy, or service descriptions, one explanation is that your work is hard to find, not hard to understand. I have not tested this for fractional roles, so treat it as a risk to check.

A professional who has served as an interim CFO, a part-time head of operations, a retained strategy advisor, and an embedded leadership coach may have done everything a buyer means by "fractional executive." The buyer does not know the words the professional used. The professional never learned the words the buyer uses. The gap is not about competence. It is a vocabulary mismatch, and it costs both sides.

What the Label Does and Does Not Claim

Adding the word to a profile is not a claim about how you prefer to work, and it does not overwrite your coaching practice or your advisory method. It is a translation layer. It tells a search that your profile is relevant to a query, and it tells a buyer skimming results that you are worth a second look.

It is also a claim of fit, so it has to be true. If you have led a function for a company on a part-time, defined basis, say so. If you have not, do not borrow the word.

Labels can commoditize. If everyone calls themselves a fractional executive, the term loses signal and the market slides toward rate competition. That risk is real at the category level. But a professional who has held the actual roles is not made generic by using the common word. The word gets you in front of the buyer. Your depth of experience is what gets you hired.

Labels get you found. Depth gets you hired.

One Check You Can Run This Week

One response to all of this is to wait: for a referral from someone who understands the shape of your work, for a client to describe you to a colleague, for the market to find language for what you do. Meanwhile buyers type a specific phrase and find a specific set of professionals. Some of them may have thinner track records. They used the word.

Search your own headline, summary, website, and service pages for "fractional" and "interim." If neither appears, ask who a buyer would find instead of you.

The coach in the opening picture has the operations background and the crisis experience a fractional COO engagement requires. What stands between her and a buyer who is searching is a word she never thought to use, in a field she never thought to fill. The buyer already decided what to type.
