A List of What You Know Is Not a Position.

A bio that lists every competency answers a question nobody asked. The buyer arrives with a problem, and the list gives her nothing to hold.

BICKER TABLE ADVISORY · AUTHORITY SERIES · ESSAY 04

Picture a fractional CFO's bio. This one is invented: "Fractional CFO | 20+ years scaling high-growth companies. Expert in financial strategy, FP&A, fundraising, M&A, and more. Previously CFO at three venture-backed companies. Open to a variety of engagements." Read it twice. Then ask what a buyer under pressure asks: what does this person solve for me, right now?

There is no answer. There is a resume.

What the List Is Doing

A competency list looks like a writing problem. It is closer to a logic problem. The executive who writes it may believe she is lowering the barrier to entry. If she names every function, every sector, and every engagement type, no buyer will feel excluded. The effect can run the other way. When no buyer feels excluded, no buyer feels found.

Here is a cost the executive rarely counts. She imagines the deals a narrow bio might lose. She does not count what each unclear line costs the buyer: time spent decoding the profile, a second search for someone whose positioning is plain, and a first call that opens with "So what exactly do you focus on?" The buyer pays that cost first. The executive pays later, in calls that never get booked and cannot be traced back to the bio. That is why the list persists long after it stops working.

Fractional work is growing, and the pool of people offering it appears to be large. Revelio Labs, a workforce data company, reported that 24 of every 1,000 new executive positions advertised in 2026 were fractional, up from nine five years earlier. Go Fractional, which operates a commercial talent platform, says its network holds more than 18,000 operators. The second figure is a company claim and not an audit. Together they suggest a buyer may have many bios to read, though that is an inference and not something either figure measures. If it holds, a long list of capabilities does not help one stand out.

Breadth Is Real, and It Has a Place

Breadth is a genuine strength. A fractional executive who has run finance through a fundraise, an acquisition, and a restructuring is more valuable than one who has seen only clean quarters. Buyers do want a leader who can cover several functions, hold a board conversation, and manage a team at once.

The mistake is where breadth shows up. When range is the headline, it reads as generalism. When range is the evidence behind a specific claim, it reads as depth. A buyer does not need to know you have done M&A before she knows what problem you solve. Once she knows, the M&A background becomes proof and stops being noise.

Profile advice says show your range. Range without a stated first problem reads as no position.

A list of what you know is not a position.

A position is a description of a situation you are known to resolve, not a description of yourself. A buyer in a specific crisis is not searching for an impressive person. She is searching for evidence that someone has seen her exact problem before. A list cannot provide that. A named problem can.

Take the same invented CFO, rewritten to state a first problem: "I step in as fractional CFO when a Series A software company has outgrown its spreadsheets and needs a board-ready monthly close, a forecast, and a fundraising model within 90 days. I work two days a week, Pacific time, and can start within 30 days." That is not a tidier version of the first bio. It is a different object. It answers who, what, when, and how before the buyer has to ask. The fundraising and M&A experience is still there, available in the conversation, where it does more work.

What Narrowing Costs

Narrowing has a price. You built your range over fifteen or twenty years, and a narrow bio shows a fraction of it. A buyer who needs help with organizational design reads a finance-focused bio and moves on, and you never get the conversation. That is true as far as it goes. A precise bio does exclude some readers at the surface. The wager is that it loses buyers you might not have won anyway and gives the right buyer a reason to reach out. I have no data on how those two trade off, so treat it as a judgment to make and not a result.

What Writing Around a Problem Requires

Name the trigger, not the function. Not "I provide financial leadership," but the moment a company's finance function stops being able to answer the questions the board is asking. That moment has a shape, and founders who have lived it may recognize the description before the second sentence ends. Recognition is what a bio is trying to produce.

State a concrete scope: a deliverable, a timeline, a time commitment, and when you can start. Details like these signal that you have done this kind of work before and know what it takes. Vague availability can read as a vague engagement.

Accept that some readers will self-select out. That is the purpose. A buyer who is not a match should not spend a whole call finding that out, and a precise bio does that filtering before a call is booked.

A Test for This Week

Take your current bio and cross out every competency you would not want to be hired for. What remains is closer to a position. Then write one sentence that names the trigger, the scope, and when you can start.
