The First Referral Problem.

A colleague calls her “a great sales leader.” The founder still has no idea whether she can fix what is broken. A referral is only as strong as the sentence it carries.

BICKER TABLE ADVISORY · AUTHORITY SERIES · ESSAY 01

Imagine a fractional CRO who spent twelve years building sales organizations. She leaves her last full-time role, posts a LinkedIn banner, and tells everyone she is going fractional. A colleague introduces her to a founder as "a great sales leader." The introduction tells the founder she is respected. It does not tell the founder whether she can fix the sales process falling apart at a company of thirty people after the first VP walked out.

The founder does not need to know whether this woman is good. She needs to know whether this woman fixes the problem she has this quarter.

A referral is only as strong as the sentence it carries.

What the Surveys Say About the Pipeline

Go Fractional, which operates a commercial fractional-talent platform, surveyed 213 fractional professionals in April 2026. Ninety-two percent said they rely on personal networks as their primary way to find clients. Ninety percent named client acquisition a challenge, and seventy percent named marketing themselves. Among those with five or more years in the work, 43 percent still named finding new clients as their primary goal.

Respondents could give more than one answer to the challenge questions.

The survey shows that finding clients is hard even for people who work through networks. It does not show why. My explanation, that the language traveling through those networks is too vague to act on, is a hypothesis.

Who Pays for the Vague Sentence

The colleague who wrote "great sales leader" risks little. She made a generous introduction and put her name behind it. The executive bears the cost, in every introduction that does not turn into a conversation. The colleague said the most accurate thing she knew how to say. Nobody ever gave her anything better.

The Reputation Trap

The people who refer you know the version of you that existed at your last company. The longer you were there and the better you did, the more fixed that description became. Picture a chief human resources officer who spent a decade at a mid-market manufacturer. She will be introduced to every new contact as "a great CHRO from the manufacturing world." It is accurate. It is also close to useless if her fractional offer is integrating people operations after an acquisition.

The standard advice is to build your network. Two hundred more connections who know you only by your old title do not double your pipeline. They give the old description two hundred more places to travel.

Specificity feels like a risk when you are the one writing it. If you name post-acquisition companies, you worry about the Series A founder who needs you just as much. A narrow description gives the founder who recognizes herself a reason to consider a call. A broad one leaves her unable to tell whether it applies to her.

The Search That Follows the Introduction

Before a founder spends an hour on a stranger, she may look the stranger up, and she may ask an AI assistant. When a buyer asks a search-enabled assistant about you without supplying your background, your public record is one source it can use. Check whether its description is accurate and which sources support it. The evidence on this is thin for fractional executives, so treat it as a risk to check and not as a finding.

The best fractional work still comes through people who know you, and I would not tell anyone to stop building a network. But a warm introduction can still lead to a search. Engagements end, and keeping a pipeline means being understood by the next buyer, including the ones who hear about you secondhand.

The Test

Write down the sentence you believe each of your last three referrers would use to describe your fractional work. Then ask each of them how they would describe it, and compare their answers with your intended description. Separately, ask an AI assistant a buyer's question without using your name, such as who to hire for the problem you solve. Record the question, the assistant, the date, the full answer, whether you appear, how you are described, and which sources it cites. One answer is a starting observation, so repeat it before you draw a conclusion.

A sentence that travels names three things: the kind of company, the problem that triggers the engagement, and what is different when you leave. "She comes in when a founder-led company has outgrown its informal sales process and needs a repeatable revenue engine before the next round" travels. "He's a great CHRO with a lot of experience" creates a moment of goodwill and expires.

A sentence on its own is a claim, so it needs proof beside it. Some of your best work sits under confidentiality, so the proof can carry a number and a situation without a name, such as a sales cycle that shortened or a team that stopped losing the same deals. Check your agreements and get the client's permission before you publish any result. Then put the sentence and its proof on a page you own. A marketplace profile gives you reach under someone else's rules.
