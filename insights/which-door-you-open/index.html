Week 2 · Tuesday series

Your AI starting point depends on which door you open.

Jamie Arias, founder and AI Systems Architect · Tuesday series for service businesses · Published on this site November 4, 2026

Find Your AI Starting Point (opens in a new tab)

Your company does not have one AI maturity level. It has five or six of them, one for every part of the business.

Marketing may already run on AI drafts. Finance still lives in spreadsheets. Operations has either not started or started and stopped. Ask whether your company uses AI, and the honest answer is: it depends which door you open.

Where your company stands with AI is not one answer. Each part of the business has its own starting point. Almost every piece of AI advice I hear assumes one starting line for the whole company. Planning as though there is one is where the real cost hides.

What the Company Average Hides

Picture a 90-person accounting firm. The marketing coordinator produces a month of social posts with AI in one afternoon. The partners still review every client file by hand. The onboarding team types the same client details into three systems. Ask that firm whether it uses AI, and the answer is yes. Plan the next AI investment on that yes, and you will buy something that suits the marketing coordinator and confuses everyone else.

Census research on AI use in US firms, drawing on survey data from November 2025 to January 2026, found that 57% of firms using AI used it in three or fewer business functions. Most AI adopters have real depth in one or two places and very little anywhere else.

That is not a failing. It is information, if you read it honestly. Look function by function, and the right first step usually becomes obvious. It is often smaller and closer to home than the advice you have been hearing.

The Cost of One Plan for Every Team

A single plan for every team costs you in three places.

The first is money. An advanced tool goes to a function that is not ready to use it. The subscription runs for a year. Nobody logs in after week three.

The second is time. A company-wide rollout suits one department and stalls in the rest. The others wait, then quietly go back to how they worked before.

The third is the one owners feel most: attention. Meetings go to debating whether the company is behind on AI instead of choosing one function and starting.

Picture a leadership meeting where one manager says the company is far behind on AI and another says it is ahead, and both are describing their own department accurately. More data will not settle that argument. It settles the moment someone says, "You are both right. These are two different starting points, and each one needs a different next move."

The Case for a Single Roadmap

The case for one company-wide roadmap is stronger than it looks. One vendor. One contract. One training push. One budget line. Everyone learns the same tool and talks about AI in the same language. Licenses are cheaper at volume, and the owner makes one hard decision instead of five.

That last point is real. A Goldman Sachs 10,000 Small Businesses survey of 1,471 owners in the program, conducted in September and October 2025, found that among owners already using AI, 47% said choosing the right tools was difficult and 45% cited a lack of technical expertise. When every tool choice feels hard, one roadmap feels like relief.

It is relief, as long as every function starts in the same place. The standard sequence is pick a platform, run a pilot, scale what works. When every team is at the same stage, that holds up. When marketing is two years ahead of operations, it produces a pilot that shines in marketing and stalls everywhere else. The hard choice did not go away. It got made once, for the most advanced team, and handed to everyone else.

My own view has a fair objection too. Maturity models are tidy and businesses are not, and labeling a team can feel like grading. Nobody wants finance told it is behind while marketing gets a trophy. So I skip the levels and use four plain sentences. They are conversation starters, not scores, and one company can say all four at once.

We use ChatGPT, and that is about it.

We bought tools, but they do not connect.

AI works inside our teams, but the teams work separately.

We want AI to take on more of the work.

The right first step depends on where you are standing.

One Line per Function

Picture an owner who comes back from a conference convinced the firm needs AI agents, then asks the operations manager how client requests move through the business today and hears three different answers. An agent needs a process clear enough to hand off. When three people describe it three ways, the first step for operations is not an agent. It is a conversation about how the work actually moves.

That conversation costs nothing, and it often produces the clearest AI decision of the year. Starting from a stage you are not at can cost months. Naming the one you are at takes an honest hour.

Individual AI productivity is not organizational intelligence. One person drafting faster is a personal win. The business gets smarter when that draft feeds a shared process and the next person in the chain does not start from scratch. That intelligence is built part by part, from where each part actually is, not from where its keenest users are.

AI does not fix broken systems, it amplifies them. It amplifies what works just as faithfully, one function at a time, when the starting point is honest.

Picture the next leadership meeting. On one page, each function has a single line: where it is starting with AI today and the one next step that fits it. Marketing will share its tested prompts with sales. Operations will map client onboarding before buying anything. Finance chooses to wait a quarter. Nobody is behind, and everyone knows their next move.

The owner chooses the starting point and the order for each function. AI does not pick its own first project, and neither should a vendor. Leave that choice open, and whichever vendor calls first will make it for you.

Your Starting Points This Week

List your four or five main functions. Beside each one, write the sentence from the four above that fits it best today. Notice how many different sentences you wrote. Then decide one thing: which function goes first, and what its next step is.

A company-wide AI average describes no department you actually run.

Seeing each function on its own terms is what makes the next AI decision simple. When each function knows where it stands, the next step is usually smaller and cheaper than anything a keynote will sell you. I would rather spend an hour mapping every team than watch an owner spend a year paying for a tool that fits one of them.

If you looked at each part of your business separately, where is each one actually starting with AI?

If you found a different answer behind every door, that is the conversation I have with owners every week. I offer a 15 to 20 minute AI pre-assessment to map where each part of your company is starting today and where AI can create the most value next.
